Chapter 9 · Strogatz §11.1–11.3

Fractal dimension

In a classical world, dimensions are whole numbers: a point is 0, a line is 1, a square is 2. But when a shape is made of smaller copies of itself, the rules change. We start by repeatedly removing the middle third of a line, then we fold a curve until it's infinitely long but bounds a finite area.

The Cantor set

Start with a solid line segment from 0 to 1. Remove the open middle third. You are left with two smaller segments. Remove the middle third of each of those. Keep going forever. What is left over?

At each step we take out a chunk, so the total length shrinks. If we do this forever, the total length goes to exactly zero. Yet there are infinitely many points left behind — not just the endpoints of the cuts! It's dust.

Drag the slider up. Each row is one more round of cutting; the pieces double in number and shrink three times faster. By n = 7 the row is 128 slivers totalling 0.058 of the original length — and the cutting has only just begun.

At step n, there are 2^n line segments, each of length (1/3)^n. Total length is (2/3)^n \to 0 as n \to \infty.

The Koch curve

Now, instead of removing the middle third, we replace it with two sides of an equilateral triangle. The shape grows spikes. At each step, every line segment turns into four smaller segments.

Since we replace 1 segment with 4 segments (each 1/3 the length), the total length gets multiplied by 4/3 at each step. By the end, its length is infinity! Yet you can draw it inside a box.

Drag the slider up and watch the readout: the length climbs past 1×, 2×, 4× the original and keeps going. By n = 4 the crinkles are smaller than the pixels — but the length is still growing.

After n folds there are 4^n segments of length 3^{-n}, so the length is (4/3)^n \to \infty as n \to \infty.

Similarity dimension

If you take a square and scale it down by a factor of 3, you need 9 copies to build the original square back (since 9 = 3^2, the dimension is 2). If you take a cube and scale it down by 3, you need 27 copies (27 = 3^3, dimension 3). What about the Koch curve? Scale it down by 3, and you need exactly 4 copies to build the original curve.

The Koch curve is self-similar: move the pointer over it to zoom 3×.

It's more than a 1D line because a normal line scaled down by 3 needs only 3 copies to rebuild it. The Koch curve needs 4. But it's less than a 2D square (which needs 9). Its dimension is a fraction!

The similarity dimension d solves N = r^d, where N is the number of copies and r is the scale factor. So d = \frac{\ln N}{\ln r}.

For Koch: N = 4, r = 3 \implies d = \frac{\ln 4}{\ln 3} \approx 1.26.

For Cantor: N = 2, r = 3 \implies d = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln 3} \approx 0.63.
