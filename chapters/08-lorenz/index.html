Lorenz: closing the loop

In 1963 Edward Lorenz boiled weather down to three equations and found they could not be forecast. This last chapter brings everything together: fixed points, instability, the butterfly effect, and at the very end, a one-dimensional map hiding inside a three-dimensional flow.

Meet Lorenz

The state is three numbers, (x, y, z), so it lives in space rather than on a plane:

\dot x = \sigma(y - x), \qquad \dot y = r x - y - x z, \qquad \dot z = x y - b z,

with \sigma = 10, b = 8/3, and a dial r for how hard the system is driven. Drag the view to turn it.

The waterwheel

Here is a machine that obeys the same equations. Water pours steadily into the top of a wheel whose cups all leak. Heavy cups on one side turn the wheel, which carries water round, which changes which side is heavy. The wheel’s state is three numbers: its spin, and two kinds of imbalance (left–right and top–bottom). After rescaling, they are exactly Lorenz’s x, y, z, and the dial r is how hard the water pours.

The map inside the flow

Lorenz noticed something about z(t): it climbs to a peak, falls, climbs to another peak, over and over. What if the size of each peak decided the next one? Call the peaks z_1, z_2, z_3, \dots and plot each one against the next.

A three-dimensional flow, and yet its peaks follow a simple one-dimensional rule, a map as in Chapter 1. Both branches of the tent are steeper than 1, so by Chapter 1’s slope rule every fixed point, and every cycle, is hollow. Nothing can settle, so the peaks never repeat. That’s the chaos, explained by a slope.

Choose fitted curve and drag the ▲ on the cobweb. It never settles. Then compare with Ch 3’s tent: a sharp peak with slopes steeper than 1, the hump that had no doubling cascade.

The Lorenz map is z_{n+1} = f(z_n). It satisfies |f'(z)| > 1 everywhere, so for any cycle z_1, \dots, z_p the multiplier is

\left|\prod_{i=1}^{p} f'(z_i)\right| > 1,

and every cycle is unstable. (Strictly, the map is a thin band rather than a curve, which is why its points have a little thickness.)

Closing the loop

Look back at the path you’ve taken:

  1. Pressing a button over and over: state, rule, time, and a fixed point you found by iterating.
  2. The slope rule: |slope| < 1 pulls in, |slope| > 1 pushes out.
  3. The butterfly effect: tiny differences doubling until the forecast fails.
  4. Doublings on the way to chaos, at a rate (4.669) shared by every rounded hump.
  5. From jumps to glides, and Euler’s method turning a flow back into a map.
  6. Tilting landscapes where valleys appear, merge and vanish.
  7. One map of every linear portrait: trace and determinant.
  8. Nonlinear portraits: a pendulum, tipping points, loops that make their own rhythm.
  9. And here, a flow in three dimensions whose peaks obey a map like the first one you ever met.

That’s the book’s arc, in the book’s vocabulary. You’re ready to read Strogatz.
