The butterfly effect

Nothing in this chapter is random. The rule is a single line of arithmetic, and you can run it as often as you like. Even so, you won’t be able to predict it for long.

Twins

The rule for this chapter is x \mapsto 4x(1-x), a hump over [0, 1] like the cos curve in Chapter 1. It has two fixed points, and both are hollow: the slope is 4 at 0 and −2 at 0.75. There’s nowhere to settle.

Start two runs almost together, one at 0.4 and its twin a ten-millionth away. Guess first.

Switch to calm: the same tiny gap, the same kind of hump, but a lower one. Now the twins slide into a filled dot together and stay there. Sensitivity belongs to the rule, not to the start.

Measure the gap

How fast do the twins separate? Plot the distance between them, |x_n - y_n|, on a log scale, where each gridline is ten times the one below.

make your prediction first

The gap roughly doubles every step. Doubling is slow at first and then very fast. Starting from a ten-millionth, 23 doublings reach 1, since 2²³ is about 8 million. That’s why the twins agree for about twenty steps and then have nothing in common.

Once the gap is as big as the hump itself, it can’t grow any more. That’s the flat ceiling.

On a log scale, steady multiplication is a straight line. The slope of the line is the multiplier per step. Tilt the ruler until it lies along the gap’s rise.

Call the gap \delta_n = y_n - x_n. It grows exponentially:

|\delta_n| \approx |\delta_0|\, e^{\lambda n}.

\lambda is the Liapunov exponent. For 4x(1-x), \lambda = \ln 2 \approx 0.693: the gap doubles each step on average. \lambda > 0 is what “chaotic” means. The gap reaches size a after about

n_{\text{horizon}} \approx \frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{a}{|\delta_0|}\ \text{steps}.

Your computer is a butterfly too. 4x(1-x) and 4x - 4x^2 are the same rule. Run both from exactly 0.4.

The forecaster

Somewhere there is a true starting value, known to every digit. You get to measure it, but only to a limited number of decimal places. Then you forecast by running the rule from your measurement. Your forecast counts as good while it stays within 0.1 of the truth.

Horizon against digits: every forecast you run adds a point

A catch: the “truth” here is itself computed with about 16 digits, so after roughly 50 steps even the truth is fiction. No computer can follow this rule exactly for long.
