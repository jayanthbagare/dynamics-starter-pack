Bifurcation & universality

Chapter 2 showed a calm hump and a chaotic one. Here the height of the hump becomes a dial, and you’ll watch order break down into chaos one doubling at a time. The doublings come at a rate given by a number that turns up for almost every hump.

Turn the dial

The rule is x \mapsto r\,x(1-x), the logistic map. The dial r sets the height of the hump. At r = 2.8 you get Chapter 2’s calm twins, and at r = 4 the chaotic ones. What happens in between?

The diagram builds itself

Instead of trying one r at a time, try them all. For each r (one thin column), run the rule until the start is forgotten, then mark every place it keeps landing. One value gives a dot, a 2-cycle gives two dots, and chaos gives a smear.

Click or drag on the diagram to choose r (the ▲). With the diagram focused, ←/→ nudge it.

Each branch is a cycle. As r grows, the cycle’s slope passes through −1: it turns hollow and splits into a cycle twice as long. One value becomes 2, then 4, then 8… The splits come faster and faster and pile up at r_\infty \approx 3.5699. Past that point is chaos, broken up by windows of order such as the period-3 window.

Zoom into any branch just before it splits and you see a miniature copy of the whole diagram. Try Inside the window: the period-3 window has its own complete cascade.

A 2-cycle is a pair p, q with f(p) = q and f(q) = p, so both are fixed points of f \circ f. The fixed point x^* = 1 - 1/r has slope

f'(x^*) = 2 - r,

which passes −1 at r = 3. That’s the first doubling. The 2-cycle is stable while |(f\circ f)'(p)| = |f'(p)\,f'(q)| < 1, and it doubles in turn when that product passes −1, at r = 1 + \sqrt 6 \approx 3.449.

Strogatz calls this picture an orbit diagram, and saves “bifurcation diagram” for flows. It’s the same idea.

Measure the doublings

The splits come faster and faster. How much faster? Zoom in, put the ▲ exactly where a branch splits, and mark it. Mark at least four.

Universality: draw your own hump

Maybe 4.669 is just a property of r\,x(1-x). Test it: draw any hump you like and use its height a as the dial. The rule becomes x \mapsto a\,h(x), where h is your hump scaled to a peak of 1.

δ, measured for your hump (updates when you change it)

Each doubling happens at a finer scale than the last. The only part of the hump the machinery “sees” at those scales is its top. Any rounded top looks the same close up, so every rounded hump gives the same δ.

A sharp top (tent) or a very flat top (flat top) looks different close up, so it gives a different answer, or no cascade at all.

Try the presets, then drag. The δ column settles to the same 4.669 unless you choose the flat top or the tent.

The Feigenbaum constant is the limiting ratio of successive gaps between doublings:

\delta = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{r_n - r_{n-1}}{r_{n+1} - r_n} = 4.669201\ldots

The table measures it with superstable parameters s_n, where the peak c of the hump lies on the 2^n-cycle (f^{2^n}(c) = c). These are easier to pin down than the doubling points, and their gaps shrink by the same δ.
