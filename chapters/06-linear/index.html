Linear systems

Now the state has two numbers, (x, y), and the phase line becomes a phase plane. The simplest flows there are linear. There are only a handful of kinds, and a single map tells you which one you have.

Matrices move the plane New to eigenvectors? Start here. Otherwise skip ahead.

A 2×2 matrix A is a rule for moving every point of the plane: (x, y) \mapsto (a x + b y,\ c x + d y). Press Transform to watch the grid move. Then drag the round tip of v and watch where Av lands.

Two rules at once

The simplest system in the plane is two copies of Chapter 4, side by side, that ignore each other:

\dot x = \lambda_1 x, \qquad \dot y = \lambda_2 y.

Each coordinate just grows or decays at its own rate. Click the portrait to start a trajectory there.

● stable (everything flows in) · ○ unstable, including saddles · ◐ neutral (a center: neither in nor out)

Eigenvectors are straight-line highways

Now let the two coordinates talk to each other: \dot x = a x + b y,\ \dot y = c x + d y. The dashed lines are the matrix’s eigenvectors, the directions it doesn’t turn.

Along an eigenvector the flow points straight along the line, so a trajectory that starts there stays there, growing or shrinking at its own rate λ. Every other trajectory is a blend of the two highways. As time goes on, the faster-growing one takes over.

If the matrix rotates everything, there are no highways at all, and trajectories spiral.

Start a trajectory on each dashed line, then one in between. Watch which highway the in-between one ends up following.

If A\mathbf v_i = \lambda_i \mathbf v_i, then e^{\lambda_i t}\mathbf v_i is a solution, and every solution is a blend:

\mathbf x(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\mathbf v_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\mathbf v_2.

The \lambda’s are the roots of the characteristic equation

\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0, \qquad \tau = a + d,\quad \Delta = ad - bc.

Complex roots \alpha \pm i\omega mean rotation at rate \omega, with growth or decay e^{\alpha t}: a spiral.

The trace–determinant map

The characteristic equation only depends on two numbers: the trace τ and the determinant Δ. So every linear system is a single point on a map, and the map’s regions name the portraits. Drag the ◆. The system here is a spring, \ddot x = \tau\,\dot x - \Delta\,x: Δ is the stiffness and −τ is the damping. The vertical axis of the portrait is the velocity.

τ is the sum of the two growth rates and Δ is their product. A negative product means one grows and one shrinks: a saddle, whatever τ is. A positive product means they have the same sign, and τ tells you which sign: stable on the left, unstable on the right. The parabola separates real rates (nodes) from complex ones (spirals).

Take the tour. The only boundaries you can cross are the Δ = 0 line, the parabola, and the upper half of the Δ-axis. Right on the Δ-axis (τ = 0) is the knife edge of centers.

\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}

\Delta < 0: saddle. \Delta > 0,\ \tau^2 > 4\Delta: node. \tau^2 < 4\Delta: spiral. \tau = 0,\ \Delta > 0: center. \tau^2 = 4\Delta: star or degenerate node. \Delta = 0: a line of fixed points. Stable when \tau < 0, unstable when \tau > 0.
