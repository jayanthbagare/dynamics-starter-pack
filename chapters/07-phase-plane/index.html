The phase plane

Chapter 6’s flows were linear, so their portraits were simple. Real systems are nonlinear. They can have several fixed points, loops, and boundaries that decide your fate. This chapter reads those portraits, starting with the one object everyone knows: a pendulum.

A pendulum and its portrait

The state of a pendulum is two numbers: its angle θ and its angular velocity ω. So its whole life is a path in the (θ, ω) plane. The angle wraps around (θ = π and −π are the same, straight up), so the left and right edges of the portrait are glued together. Drag the bob and let go, or click the portrait to set a state.

The highlighted curve, the separatrix, runs from upside-down to upside-down. Inside it the pendulum swings back and forth; outside it goes over the top and keeps going. Add damping and every loop becomes a spiral into the bottom: Chapter 6’s center turning into a stable spiral.

Careful with centers. Linearization settles saddles, nodes and spirals. A center is a knife edge (Chapter 6), and the neglected nonlinear terms can tip it into a slow spiral either way. The pendulum really does keep its loops, but for a different reason: with no damping it conserves energy, and each loop is an energy contour.

Every smooth flow is linear up close. So near each fixed point, Chapter 6’s map tells you what you’ll see, except in the borderline cases on its special lines.

Raise the zoom until the two portraits are indistinguishable. Try it on both fixed points.

Write the distance from the fixed point as \boldsymbol\eta = \mathbf x - \mathbf x^*. Then

\dot{\boldsymbol\eta} \approx J\,\boldsymbol\eta, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x & \partial f/\partial y \\ \partial g/\partial x & \partial g/\partial y \end{pmatrix}_{\mathbf x^*}.

For the pendulum, J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\tfrac gL\cos\theta^* & -b \end{pmatrix}: a center (or stable spiral) at the bottom, a saddle at the top.

Competition: nullclines and a tipping point

Two species share one resource. Each grows on its own until it fills its space, and each crowds the other:

\dot x = x(1 - x - a\,y), \qquad \dot y = y(0.8 - y - c\,x).

The dashed curves are nullclines: on one, x isn’t changing (ẋ = 0); on the other, y isn’t (ẏ = 0). Fixed points are where they cross. Click to start trajectories.

Limit cycles

A system that pushes small motions up and drags large motions down (like a heart cell, or an old valve circuit) settles into a rhythm of its own. Here it is as the van der Pol oscillator, \ddot x - \mu(1 - x^2)\dot x + x = 0.

Compare Chapter 6’s center: a whole family of loops, each one ignoring its neighbours. A limit cycle is a single, isolated loop that pulls its neighbours in. It’s the shape of a sustained, self-made rhythm.

Hopf: a loop is born

How does a rhythm start? Turn a dial μ on a system that spirals into a fixed point. At μ = 0 the spiral stops pulling in, and a small loop appears. In polar coordinates the system is \dot r = \mu r - r^3,\ \dot\theta = \omega.
